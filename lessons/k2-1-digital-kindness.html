🤝 Digital Kindness & Screen Manners

Grade Level: Kindergarten – 2nd Grade
Duration: 15 minutes
Topic: Online Etiquette
📋 Standards Alignment
ISTE Standards for Students Digital Citizen 2c: Demonstrate good digital citizenship by treating others with respect and keeping personal information private.
Common Core Standards SL.K.1 / SL.1.1 / SL.2.1: Participate in collaborative conversations about familiar topics.
SEL Standards Social Awareness: Understand respect for others and recognize different perspectives.
🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

  • Identify kind and unkind behaviors online
  • Understand that screens are tools we use, not bad things
  • Describe simple digital manners (taking turns, listening, being kind)
  • Practice treating online friends the way they treat real friends
📦 Materials Needed
  • Projector or screen to show examples
  • Chart paper or whiteboard
  • Printable activity sheets (included below)
  • Markers or colored pencils
  • Optional: tablets/computers for hands-on practice

📖 Full Lesson Plan
Opening: The Kindness Hook (3 minutes)

Say to students:

"Today we're going to talk about something important: being kind online. Has anyone ever played a game on a tablet or watched a video online? Yes? Well, when we're using screens and talking to people online, we can be kind—just like we're kind to our friends at recess. Let me show you what that looks like."

Show real-world scenario: Describe or show a picture of kids playing an online game together, chatting nicely, taking turns, and being helpful. Then show the opposite—kids being mean, ignoring others, not listening.

Pro Tip: Use simple, relatable language. Avoid scary language. This age group responds well to concrete examples they can visualize.
Teach: What Does Digital Kindness Look Like? (5 minutes)

Key concept: Screens are tools we use to talk and play, just like the playground. The same rules apply!

Teach these "Screen Manners" together on the board:

  • Use kind words: Say "please," "thank you," and "I like that idea!"
  • Take turns: Listen when others talk. Don't interrupt (even online!).
  • Include others: If someone wants to join your game, say "yes!"
  • Don't share mean words: If someone is mean, tell a grown-up—don't be mean back.
  • Ask before sharing: Don't share pictures of your friends without asking first.

Connect to what they know: "Remember when we learned about being kind on the playground? Online kindness is the same thing!"

Discuss: Interactive Questions (3 minutes)
❓ Question 1: "If you were playing a game online and someone said something mean, what would you do?"
❓ Question 2: "How do you think someone feels if they read a mean message online?"
❓ Question 3: "What's one kind thing you could say to someone online?"
Tip for Facilitators: Elicit responses from multiple students. Write their ideas on the board. Affirm all answers. If a student suggests something unkind, gently redirect: "That might make them sad. What could we say instead that's kind?"
Activity: Kindness Role-Play (3 minutes)

📌 Scenario Role-Play

Option A (No devices needed):

Pair up students. One student pretends to be playing an online game. The other practices saying kind things to them. Examples:

  • "Great move! You're good at this game!"
  • "Can I join your game? Please?"
  • "I like the way you helped me."

Option B (With devices):

If students have access to tablets or a classroom communication app, have them practice sending kind messages to a peer (under teacher supervision). Keep messages short and simple.

Wrap-Up & Key Takeaway (1 minute)

Say to students:

"Being kind online is just as important as being kind to your friends at school. When you use screens, remember: use kind words, take turns, and include others. If someone is mean, tell a grown-up. You're all doing a great job thinking about digital kindness!"

Send home the parent guide below so families can reinforce the lesson at home.

💡 Implementation Notes
Differentiation
  • For Younger Students (K-Early 1st): Use picture cards showing kind vs. unkind behavior. Keep language very simple.
  • For Advanced Learners: Ask them to think of more complex scenarios: "What if your friend posted a picture of you without asking? What would you do?"
  • For ELL Students: Pre-teach vocabulary (kind, mean, game, online). Use visuals. Pair with a bilingual peer if possible.
Anticipated Challenges & Solutions
  • Challenge: "But I don't have a screen at home!"
    Solution: Normalize this. Say: "That's OK! This lesson is about the idea of being kind. These rules apply anywhere—at school, with friends, or if you use screens."
  • Challenge: A student shares that they were treated unkindly online.
    Solution: Listen, validate, and offer support. Say: "Thank you for telling me. That must have felt sad. It's not your fault. Let's tell a parent/counselor who can help."
  • Challenge: Students get distracted or silly during role-play.
    Solution: Redirect with humor: "Great energy! Let's practice using kind words now."
Connection to Family & Home

Send the parent guide home and encourage families to:

  • Model kind online behavior when they use screens around their child
  • Talk about digital kindness during family time
  • Praise their child when they see them being kind online
📧 Parent Send-Home Guide
Digital Kindness at Home

What We Learned in Class

This week, we talked about being kind online—whether that's playing games, watching videos, or chatting with friends. The same rules of kindness that apply at school and home apply when using screens too!

Screen Manners We Practice

  • Use kind words: Say "please," "thank you," and "great idea!"
  • Take turns: Let others talk. Listen when it's their turn.
  • Include others: Be friendly to people online, just like at recess.
  • Don't share mean words: If someone is mean, tell a grown-up.
  • Ask before sharing: Always ask permission before posting photos of friends.

Try This at Home

1. Model kindness: Let your child see you being kind online. Talk out loud about the nice words you're using.

2. Play together: If your child plays an online game, sit with them and notice kind moments. Praise them: "I liked how you helped your teammate!"

3. Talk about feelings: Ask: "How did that person's kind message make you feel?" or "What could we say if someone was mean?"

4. Set a good example: Put your own phone away during family time. Kids notice!

Red Flags to Watch For

If your child ever experiences mean messages, unkind behavior, or anything that makes them uncomfortable online:

  • Listen without judgment
  • Don't blame them
  • Take a screenshot or save the message
  • Report it to the app or platform
  • Contact the school if it involves classmates

Questions?

Please reach out to your teacher with any questions or concerns about digital kindness, screen time, or online safety at home.
