🚨 Stranger Danger: Who Can You Trust Online?

Grade Level: Kindergarten – 2nd Grade
Duration: 15 minutes
Topic: Online Safety & Strangers
📋 Standards Alignment
ISTE Standards for Students Digital Citizen 2a: Cultivate and manage their digital identity and reputation and are aware of the permanence of their actions in the digital world.
Common Core Standards SL.K.2 / SL.1.2 / SL.2.2: Ask and answer questions to seek help or clarify something that is not understood.
SEL Standards Responsible Decision-Making: Demonstrate awareness of personal safety and know when and how to ask for help.
🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

  • Understand that not everyone online is who they say they are
  • Identify trusted adults in their life (parents, teachers, school counselor)
  • Know that it's okay to tell a trusted adult if something feels wrong
  • Practice saying "no" to requests from strangers online
📦 Materials Needed
  • Whiteboard or chart paper
  • Markers
  • Pictures of trusted adults (optional)
  • Printable activity sheets
  • Simple picture cards or puppets for role-play
⏱️ Lesson Timeline (15 minutes)
Opening: 3 minutes

Hook with a relatable scenario about online strangers.

Teach: 5 minutes

Explain what a "stranger online" is and why it matters.

Discuss: 3 minutes

Identify trusted adults and practice what to do.

Activity: 3 minutes

Role-play scenarios and practice saying "no."

Wrap-up: 1 minute

Reinforce that it's safe to tell a trusted adult.

📖 Full Lesson Plan
Opening: The Online Stranger (3 minutes)

Say to students:

"Imagine you're playing a game online, and someone you don't know says 'hi!' They say they're also a kid, and they want to be your friend. What do you think? Can you tell who they really are just by looking at a screen?"

Key idea: You can't always know who people really are online. Someone might say they're your age, but they could be anyone.

Tone matters: Don't make this scary. Make it matter-of-fact, like street safety. "Just like we hold hands before crossing the street, we ask grown-ups before talking to people online."
Teach: Strangers Online (5 minutes)

Three big ideas:

  • Idea 1: "People online aren't always who they say they are. A stranger might say they're a kid, but they might be a grown-up."
  • Idea 2: "It's not your fault if someone online is being sneaky. Grown-ups know this can happen, and we want to help keep you safe."
  • Idea 3: "Your job is to tell a trusted adult if something feels funny or weird. You're not in trouble. We're here to help."

What to teach them to do:

  • ✅ If someone online asks you personal questions (your name, where you live, your school), tell a grown-up
  • ✅ If someone asks to be your friend and you don't know them, ask a grown-up first
  • ✅ If something online makes you feel yucky or scared, tell a grown-up right away
Discuss: Who Are Your Trusted Adults? (3 minutes)

Ask students to identify trusted adults in their life:

❓ Question 1: "Who is a trusted grown-up in your life? (mom, dad, grandparent, teacher, counselor)"

Write their answers on the board. Make a visual "trusted adults" list that they can see.

❓ Question 2: "What if something online makes you feel weird or sad? What would you do?"
❓ Question 3: "Is it okay to tell a grown-up if you think someone online is not being honest?"

Affirm all answers. Key message: "Yes! It's always okay to tell. You won't get in trouble."

Activity: "Safe or Not Safe?" Role-Play (3 minutes)

🎭 Scenario Practice

Read each scenario. Have students say "Safe!" or "Tell a grown-up!"

  1. Scenario 1: "A kid in your school asks to play a game with you online." → SAFE (you know them)
  2. Scenario 2: "Someone online says 'Hi! I'm 8 years old and want to be your friend. What's your name and where do you live?'" → TELL A GROWN-UP
  3. Scenario 3: "You're playing a game and someone sends you a message that makes you feel yucky." → TELL A GROWN-UP
  4. Scenario 4: "Your mom helps you video chat with your grandma." → SAFE (trusted adult is there)
  5. Scenario 5: "Someone online says 'Don't tell your parents about this. It's our secret.'" → TELL A GROWN-UP RIGHT NOW

Pro tip for teachers: Role-play these with puppets or stuffed animals if it helps. Younger kids respond well to dramatization.

Wrap-Up & Key Takeaway (1 minute)

Say to students:

"Remember: Online, you can't always tell if someone is really who they say they are. If something feels wrong or someone asks personal questions, you tell a trusted grown-up. You won't get in trouble. We're here to keep you safe. You're all doing great!"

💡 Implementation Notes
Differentiation
  • Younger Students (K-Early 1st): Use very simple language. Focus on "tell a grown-up" as the main rule. Use pictures instead of words.
  • Advanced Learners: Dig deeper into why people might not be honest online. What motivates them? (Curiosity is good—nurture it.)
  • Anxious Students: Reassure them. Make it clear: "This is rare. Most people online are good. You're learning to be safe, which is smart."
Handling Disclosures

If a student says something happened to them online:

  • Stay calm and thank them for telling you
  • Don't express shock or alarm (they'll feel blamed)
  • Listen without judgment
  • Say: "This is not your fault. I'm so glad you told me."
  • Document what they said (write it down after)
  • Report to school counselor/admin immediately
  • Follow mandatory reporting procedures

📧 Parent Send-Home Guide
Online Strangers & How to Keep Your Child Safe

What We Learned in Class

This week, we talked about an important safety rule: Not everyone online is who they say they are. We taught students to recognize strangers online and know when to ask for help from a trusted adult.

The Three Key Rules

  1. You can't always tell who people really are online. Someone might say they're a kid your age, but they could be anyone.
  2. It's not your fault if someone is being sneaky. Adults know this happens, and we want to help keep you safe.
  3. Tell a trusted grown-up if something feels wrong or weird. You won't get in trouble. We're here to help.

Red Flags at Home

Tell a grown-up immediately if:

  • Someone online asks your name, age, where you live, or what school you go to
  • Someone online wants to be your friend but you don't know them in real life
  • Someone sends you messages that make you uncomfortable
  • Someone online asks you to keep it a secret from your parents
  • Someone asks for pictures or videos of you

What You Can Do at Home

1. Know what your child is doing online. Ask: "Who are you talking to? What game are you playing?"

2. Set rules together. Example: "You can only talk to friends you know from school."

3. Make it safe to tell you. If something happens, respond with: "Thank you for telling me. You're not in trouble. I'm proud of you."

4. Model trust. Don't punish them for disclosing. They need to feel safe coming to you.

Questions?

If you have concerns about your child's online safety, please reach out to your teacher or school counselor.
