🎣 Spotting Scams & Fake Messages

Grade Level: 3rd – 5th Grade
Duration: 45 minutes
Topic: Critical Thinking & Online Safety
📋 Standards Alignment
ISTE Standards for Students Digital Citizen 2b: Engage constructively in online communities and question and critically evaluate online information.
Common Core Standards RI.3.8 / RI.4.8 / RI.5.8: Describe the logical connection between sentences and paragraphs; evaluate information for accuracy.
SEL Standards Responsible Decision-Making: Develop critical thinking skills to evaluate credibility and trustworthiness.
🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

  • Identify warning signs of phishing, scams, and fake messages
  • Recognize common scam tactics (urgency, too good to be true, requests for money/info)
  • Evaluate online messages for credibility
  • Respond appropriately to suspicious messages (don't click, tell a grown-up)
📦 Materials Needed
  • Printed examples of fake messages/emails/ads (screenshots or handouts)
  • Real examples of phishing emails (sanitized, from trusted sources)
  • Whiteboard/chart paper for Red Flags list
  • Handout: "Scam or Real?" activity (provided below)
  • Post-its or markers for annotation
⏱️ Lesson Timeline (45 minutes)
Opening: 5 minutes

Show examples of suspicious vs. real messages. Spark curiosity about "fake hunters."

Teach: 10 minutes

Teach common scam tactics and red flags. Build the "Red Flags" list together.

Guided Practice: 15 minutes

Students analyze message examples. Identify red flags using a checklist.

Discussion: 10 minutes

Share findings. Discuss what they'd do if they got a scam message.

Wrap-up: 5 minutes

Three rules: Don't click, don't respond, tell a grown-up.

📖 Full Lesson Plan
Opening: The Fake Hunter Challenge (5 minutes)

Show two messages on the board (or screen):

Message 1 (Fake): "Congratulations! You won a free iPhone! Click here to claim it now! Expires today!"

Message 2 (Real): "Hi! I'm reminding you that your library book is due tomorrow. Return it to avoid a late fee."

Ask students: "Which one looks fake? How can you tell?"

Reveal: "Today you're going to become 'scam detectives.' Your job is to find the red flags that tell you if a message is a scam or fake."

Teach: Common Scam Tactics (10 minutes)

Teach these red flags together. Write them on a visible chart:

  • 🚨 URGENCY: "Click now!" "Expires today!" "Act fast!" Scammers want you to act without thinking.
  • 🚨 TOO GOOD TO BE TRUE: Free prizes, money, gifts. Real companies don't give away things for nothing.
  • 🚨 ASKING FOR PERSONAL INFO: Passwords, credit card numbers, real names, addresses. Real companies never ask this via message.
  • 🚨 SUSPICIOUS LINKS: Links that don't look right. Misspelled URLs. "Click here" without showing the actual link.
  • 🚨 SENDER LOOKS WEIRD: Email address that doesn't match the company name. "paypa1.com" instead of "paypal.com" (with a "1" instead of "l").
  • 🚨 REQUESTS FOR MONEY: If someone online asks for money, it's almost always a scam.
  • 🚨 POOR GRAMMAR/SPELLING: Real companies proofread. Many scams have typos.

Real-world examples:

  • "You've won a prize!" (You never entered a contest.)
  • "Update your account now!" (You don't have an account with that company.)
  • "Confirm your password." (Real companies never ask for passwords via email/message.)
Guided Practice: Scam Detective Activity (15 minutes)

🔍 Analyze Message Examples

Provide students with 4-5 message examples (see handout below). For each one, they:

  1. Read the message
  2. Check off any red flags from the list
  3. Decide: SCAM or REAL?
  4. Write why

Example for students:

Message: "Hi! Your Amazon package is delayed. Click here to track it: amaz0n-tracking.net"

Red Flags: Suspicious sender, fake link (amaz0n vs amazon), urgency

Answer: SCAM

Teacher role: Circulate. Ask: "What do you notice? Why does that look fake? What would a real message look like?"

Discussion: Share Findings (10 minutes)

Go through each message example as a class:

❓: "What red flags did you find in this message?"
❓: "What would you do if you got this message?"
❓: "Why do you think scammers use these tricks?"

Deeper thinking questions:

  • "If something seems too good to be true, what does that probably mean?"
  • "Why don't real companies ask for passwords or personal info in messages?"
  • "Why is urgency a red flag? What are they trying to make you do?"
Wrap-Up: Three Rules (5 minutes)

Post these three rules where all students can see them:

🛑 IF YOU GET A SCAM MESSAGE:

1. DON'T CLICK any links

2. DON'T RESPOND to the message

🎯 3. TELL A GROWN-UP right away

Say: "You're now certified scam detectives! If something looks fishy, trust your gut. Tell a grown-up. Better safe than sorry!"

💡 Implementation Notes
Differentiation
  • Younger/Struggling: Use 2-3 very obvious scam examples. Highlight red flags in color.
  • Advanced: Use more subtle scams (typos so small they're hard to notice). Ask: "How could scammers be more convincing?"
Real-World Connection

If appropriate, share (anonymized) examples of scams YOU or the school has received. Kids love knowing this happens to adults too, and it normalizes reporting.

📧 Parent Send-Home Guide
Raising a Critical Thinker: Spotting Online Scams

What We Learned in Class

This week, we taught students to recognize phishing scams, fake messages, and suspicious links online. The goal: develop critical thinking skills and skepticism about what they see.

Seven Red Flags of a Scam

  1. URGENCY: "Click now!" "Limited time!" "Act fast!"
  2. TOO GOOD TO BE TRUE: Free prizes, money, or gifts
  3. ASKING FOR PERSONAL INFO: Passwords, credit cards, names, addresses
  4. SUSPICIOUS LINKS: Misspelled URLs or links that don't look right
  5. STRANGE SENDER: Email/username that doesn't match the real company
  6. REQUESTS FOR MONEY: If someone online asks for money, it's likely a scam
  7. POOR GRAMMAR/SPELLING: Real companies proofread; many scams have errors

The Three Rules

If your child gets a suspicious message, tell them:

  1. DON'T CLICK any links
  2. DON'T RESPOND to the message
  3. TELL A GROWN-UP right away

How to Practice at Home

1. Show them a scam email. Ask: "Does this look real? What's suspicious about it?"

2. Ask before clicking. If they want to click a link, ask: "Do you know where this takes you? Is it from a company you trust?"

3. Make it safe to report. If they fall for a scam or click something suspicious, don't punish. Instead: "Thank you for telling me. Let's figure out what to do together."

If Your Child Has Clicked a Suspicious Link

  • Don't panic. One click isn't always dangerous.
  • Close the page immediately.
  • Check your credit card statements for fraud.
  • If they gave their password, change it immediately.
  • Report it to the platform or company.
