Can I Trust an AI Detector?

AI detectors can sometimes provide useful clues, but a detector score is not proof that something was created by AI.

Last reviewed: September 2026

What is this?

AI detectors are tools designed to estimate whether text, images, audio or video may have been generated or manipulated using AI.

That sounds simple.

It isn't.

Detection is an ongoing technical challenge because the systems generating AI content keep changing, the content can be edited, and real-world material doesn't always look like the examples a detector was tested on.

So, do AI detectors work?

Sometimes.

But that's very different from saying they can reliably determine the truth every time.

NIST's 2026 deepfake work reports that current detection systems can experience a 45–50% drop in accuracy when moving from academic benchmarks to operational environments.

Text detectors have their own problems.

Stanford researchers tested several GPT detectors and found that they incorrectly classified 61.22% of the examined TOEFL essays written by non-native English speakers as AI-generated. The researchers warned about reliability and bias, particularly when detector results could be used to accuse someone of misconduct.

That doesn't mean every detector is useless.

It means:

A detector result is a clue, not a verdict.

What should I know?

Two mistakes are especially important.

False positive

The detector says something was generated by AI when a person actually created it.

False negative

The detector says something was created by a person when AI actually generated it.

Both matter.

A false negative can allow manipulated content to slip through.

A false positive can wrongly accuse a real person of lying, cheating or creating fake material.

What about student work?

This is especially important in education.

A score like:

"92% likely AI-generated"

may look scientific and definitive.

It isn't the same thing as proof.

Detector performance varies by tool, content, model, language and circumstances.

Because false positives exist, an AI-detector score should not by itself be treated as proof that a student, employee or writer used AI.

The Stanford research is particularly important here because it demonstrated how detector errors can disproportionately affect non-native English writers.

What about photos and video?

The same basic rule applies.

Detection tools can be part of an investigation.

They aren't the whole investigation.

NIST's current deepfake work specifically tests detectors against adversarial attacks, face and body swaps, context manipulation and more realistic operational conditions because laboratory performance does not necessarily predict performance in the real world.

What choices do I have?

If a detector flags something, don't stop there.

Ask:

  • Where did this content come from?
  • Can I find the original?
  • Is there provenance information?
  • Are there independent copies?
  • Does the supposed source confirm it?
  • What other evidence supports the claim?

If you're evaluating someone's work, what other evidence exists besides the detector score?

What can I do?

Think of detection tools as one layer of evidence.

DigitalCap's verification framework uses these layers:

  1. SOURCE – Where did it originate?
  2. PROVENANCE – Is there reliable information about its history or creation?
  3. CORROBORATION – Does independent evidence support it?
  4. CONTEXT – Is it being presented with the correct date, location and meaning?
  5. CONTENT CLUES – Does anything inside the content raise questions?
  6. DETECTION TOOLS – What do technical tools suggest?

Notice where the detector is.

It's part of the process, not the final judge.

Put it in context

We naturally want a tool that can simply tell us:

REAL

or

AI

That would be convenient.

Right now, reality is more complicated.

AI detection can provide information worth considering, but important decisions shouldn't rest on a detector score alone.

A detector result is a clue, not a verdict.
