AI & the Environment: Data Centers, Energy & Water

AI runs on real computers in real data centers. That means it uses electricity, cooling systems and infrastructure. The impact is real, but simple viral numbers rarely tell the whole story.

Reviewed September 2026 · Sources

The quick version

What you need to know:

AI runs on physical computers in data centers using real electricity and water

It's not virtual or free. Data centers consume energy for processing, storage, networks, and cooling systems.

Data-center electricity use was ~1.5% of global consumption in 2024

Projections estimate ~3% by 2030. But this includes all data services (banking, streaming, email, etc.), not just AI.

Data-center electricity use ≠ AI electricity use

AI is one of the fastest-growing loads, but data centers supported websites, storage, and business systems long before today's AI boom.

The real picture is complex, not simple viral numbers

Future demand depends on AI adoption, hardware efficiency, electricity sources, water availability, and infrastructure capacity. Regional impacts vary significantly.

How data centers and AI work

What is a data center?

AI does not actually live in "the cloud." AI systems run on physical computers housed in data centers.

Data centers also support many other digital services, including: websites, cloud storage, streaming, banking, search, social media, gaming, video calls, business systems, and traditional computing.

AI is one growing part of that infrastructure.

Why does AI use electricity?

Computers need electricity to run processors, store information, move information across networks, operate supporting equipment, and cool equipment.

Some AI workloads use specialized, high-performance chips that require substantial electricity. Large AI-focused data centers can have much higher power demands than conventional facilities.

How much electricity do data centers use?

2024 Global Data-Center Electricity Use

The International Energy Agency estimates that data centers used about:

415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024

That was about:

approximately 1.5% of global electricity consumption.

IEA 2030 Projection (Base Case)

The IEA's base-case projection estimates that global data-center electricity use could reach roughly:

945 terawatt-hours by 2030

or just under:

3% of global electricity consumption.

Important caveat

These are projections, not guarantees. Future demand depends on things including: AI adoption, hardware efficiency, software efficiency, demand for other digital services, data-center construction, electricity availability, and infrastructure constraints.

Is all of that AI?

No. This distinction matters.

Data centers existed long before today's generative AI boom. They support enormous amounts of non-AI computing. AI is now one of the most important drivers of growth, particularly because AI workloads increasingly use high-performance accelerated servers.

Key distinction

Data-center electricity use is not the same thing as AI electricity use.

What about the United States?

The impact can be much larger locally than the global percentage suggests.

A Berkeley Lab report published in June 2026 estimates that data centers could account for approximately:

11.8% of total U.S. electricity use by 2030

in its reference case.

Its scenario range is approximately:

9.5% to 15.3%.

This is a projection

This is a projection with substantial uncertainty. It is not a prediction that exactly 11.8% will occur.

Why Local Impacts Matter

Data centers are geographically concentrated. A large facility can create substantial new electricity demand in a particular:

  • city
  • county
  • utility territory
  • grid region

That can matter for:

  • grid planning
  • new transmission
  • electricity generation
  • infrastructure
  • utility investment
  • community planning

So a relatively small share of global electricity can still create significant local effects.

What about water?

Some data centers use water directly in cooling systems. Water may also be used indirectly to generate the electricity that powers a data center.

But the amount varies enormously. Factors include:

  • cooling system
  • server efficiency
  • location
  • climate
  • power source
  • server utilization
  • infrastructure efficiency
  • age of equipment
  • workload

A 2025 Berkeley Lab-led study found that workload-level water consumption could vary by more than 10,000 times depending on these factors.

Why this matters

That makes universal claims about the water used by one AI prompt extremely difficult to defend.

Viral Claims About Water

You may have heard:

"Every AI prompt uses a bottle of water."

What's actually happening?

AI and data centers can use water. But there is no single universal amount of water used by every prompt. The amount associated with a task can vary dramatically depending on:

  • which model is used
  • what kind of task it is
  • where the computation happens
  • what hardware runs it
  • how efficiently the server operates
  • how the facility is cooled
  • how the electricity is generated

A short text request, an image generation request, a long reasoning task and model training are not equivalent activities.

Key insight

Using AI is not one uniform activity.

Cooling and tradeoffs

Does every data center use huge amounts of water? No.

Cooling systems differ. Some use:

  • evaporative cooling
  • closed-loop systems
  • air cooling
  • liquid cooling

Some facilities are designed specifically to reduce or eliminate water use for cooling. There can also be tradeoffs. A cooling design that reduces water use may affect electricity use differently, depending on the system and climate.

So the question isn't simply:

"Does this data center use water?"

It is:

"How much water, where, under what conditions, and compared with which alternative?"

Does AI's environmental impact matter?

AI and data-center growth are creating real:

  • electricity demand
  • infrastructure needs
  • water considerations
  • emissions impacts
  • local planning challenges

Ignoring those impacts would not make sense. But neither would treating every AI use as environmentally identical.

Can AI Help With Environmental Problems?

Potentially. The International Energy Agency identifies possible uses of AI for:

  • energy-system optimization
  • electricity-grid management
  • renewable-energy forecasting
  • efficiency improvements
  • industrial optimization
  • emissions reduction
  • scientific research

AI may help reduce energy use or emissions in some applications. That does not cancel out the resources required to operate AI. Both things can be true.

Balanced perspective

AI has an environmental footprint and AI may also be useful for solving environmental and energy problems.

Does AI Solve Climate Change?

No. The International Energy Agency describes AI as a potentially useful tool, not a replacement for energy or climate policy.

AI cannot eliminate the need for:

  • cleaner energy
  • efficient infrastructure
  • grid investment
  • responsible planning
  • better equipment
  • policy
  • industry decisions
What can individuals realistically do?

You can use AI thoughtfully. That might mean:

  • not generating things you don't actually need
  • using simpler tools when they work just as well
  • choosing efficient options when they are available
  • keeping devices and services you use in perspective
  • learning about the companies and infrastructure behind your tools

But Here's the Bigger Picture

Individual prompts are only one small part of the larger picture. Some of the biggest environmental choices happen at the level of:

  • data-center design
  • hardware efficiency
  • cooling
  • model efficiency
  • electricity generation
  • facility location
  • grid planning
  • corporate investment
  • regulation and public policy

Important perspective

Environmental responsibility should not be reduced to making individual users feel guilty about asking AI a question.

Put it in context

You don't need to choose between:

  • "AI is destroying the environment."
  • "AI's environmental impact doesn't matter."

Neither gives you much useful information. A better understanding is:

  • AI uses real resources.
  • Use varies enormously.
  • Data centers also support many non-AI services.
  • AI is contributing significantly to new demand.
  • Local impacts can be larger than global averages suggest.
  • Efficiency choices matter.
  • Energy sources matter.
  • Cooling choices matter.
  • Industry and policy decisions matter.

Core insight

AI has a real-world footprint. You don't have to stop using it to use it thoughtfully.

And you do not need to accept a viral number just because it sounds precise.

Remember

A precise number isn't automatically an accurate number.

When someone tells you exactly how much water or electricity "one AI prompt" uses, ask:

  • Which model?
  • Which task?
  • Which data center?
  • Which cooling system?
  • Which electricity source?
  • When was it measured?

That context matters.

Final thought

AI has a real-world footprint. Understanding it is more useful than either ignoring it or exaggerating it.
