Secure an Account

Account security typically involves several layers of protection that work together.

Key protections include:

  • Strong, unique passwords: A password that's long and different from your other accounts.
  • Multi-factor or two-factor authentication: A second verification step (code from an app, text message, security key) even if someone learns your password.
  • Recovery options: A recovery email or phone number you can use if you lose access to the account.
  • Active sessions review: Checking where your account is logged in and removing access you don't recognize.
  • Connected apps and permissions: Reviewing and removing third-party apps that no longer need access.

Review Privacy & Permissions

Privacy settings control who can see, contact, and access your information. Different platforms use different terminology, but the core ideas are similar.

Common privacy areas to review:

  • Account visibility: Whether your account is public or private, and who can see your posts and profile.
  • Contact and messaging: Who can message you directly, and whether messages from unknown people go to a separate folder.
  • Location sharing: Whether your location appears in posts or is shared with specific people.
  • Tags and mentions: Whether others can tag you in posts and whether tagged content requires your approval.
  • Connected apps: Third-party apps that have access to your account or data.
  • Device permissions: Which apps on your phone can access camera, microphone, contacts, photos, or location.
  • Discovery and contact syncing: Whether others can find your account by phone number or email, and whether contact syncing is enabled.

Setting Up a New Device

You don't need to figure everything out at once. Start with these areas in order, using the DigitalCap guidance that's already available.

1. Secure the device and account

A new device is a good time to review device security and account protections. This includes the device passcode, PIN, or biometric lock; account password and multi-factor authentication; recovery options; and software updates.

Review account security →

2. Review privacy, permissions, and device controls

Review who can see and contact the account, location sharing, and app permissions. Most devices also offer built-in controls for screen time, content, purchases, and app access.

Review privacy and device settings →

3. Check apps before adding them

Before installing apps or games, use App Check to understand what an app does, what to watch for, and what settings may be relevant.

4. Decide what expectations or boundaries make sense

Different situations call for different expectations. Consider what matters for downloads, purchases, notifications, location sharing, privacy, screen time, and when and how the device is used.

Review a Device Currently in Use

If the device is already in use, you don't have to start over. Review the areas below and focus first on anything that doesn't match your needs or the situation.

1. Review privacy and who can contact the account

Start by checking who can see the account, who can message it, whether location is shared, and which app permissions are currently enabled.

Review privacy settings → or check a specific app

2. Review the device controls already in place

Most devices offer built-in controls for screen time, content, purchases, and app access. Review what's currently configured and decide whether the current setup still fits your needs or the situation.

3. Revisit expectations and boundaries

A device set up months or years ago may no longer match current expectations. Review what makes sense around downloads, purchases, notifications, location sharing, privacy, and what to do if something online feels unsafe or uncomfortable.

If you're reviewing this device with a household and want guidance on family device rules, Family Device Rules can help you think through shared expectations.

4. Notice changes that may be worth checking in on

Changes in behavior, sleep, privacy, online friendships, or device habits can sometimes prompt a conversation. Context matters, and one change by itself does not necessarily mean something is wrong.
