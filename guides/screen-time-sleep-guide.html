Understanding Screen Time & Sleep
Screen time isn't all the same. Learn how digital use can affect sleep and daily life, then decide what works for you or your family.
SCREEN TIME ISN'T JUST A NUMBER

The same amount of screen time can involve very different activities and purposes. Someone might spend two hours on homework, video calls with family, creating music, gaming with friends, learning a skill, or watching videos. Each experience is different.

Instead of only asking "How many hours?" it can be more useful to consider:

Questions to think about:
• What is the screen being used for?
• How does the person feel during and after?
• Is it interfering with sleep?
• Is it crowding out responsibilities or activities that matter?
• Is stopping easy when someone needs or wants to stop?
• Is the experience useful, enjoyable, stressful, supportive, or upsetting?

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends focusing on the quality and context of digital-media interactions rather than only total screen time. Digital media is used for school, work, connecting with friends and family, games, social media, entertainment, hobbies, learning, and more.

HOW DIGITAL USE CAN AFFECT SLEEP

Research shows that digital media use can affect how and when people sleep. Several factors may contribute, and they often work together:

Time Displacement
Scrolling, gaming, watching, chatting, or other activities can push bedtime later simply by taking up time when sleep would otherwise happen.
Content and Cognitive Arousal
Exciting, stressful, emotional, or highly engaging content may make it harder to wind down and relax before sleep.
Notifications and Alerts
Sounds, vibrations, and the urge to check alerts can interrupt sleep or make it harder to fall asleep.
Device Availability
A device within reach can make checking it during the night more likely, interfering with sleep duration and quality.
Light Exposure
Light emitted by screens, including blue-wavelength light, can affect melatonin secretion and circadian timing. However, light is only one part of the picture—time, content, notifications, and device access also matter.

These factors often interact, and individual people experience them differently. Not everyone is affected the same way.

WHAT YOU CAN TRY

Since different factors affect sleep, experimenting with different changes can help identify what makes a difference for you or your household. These are options, not requirements:

Try a quieter wind-down period before bed without screens or notifications.
Consider putting your phone on Do Not Disturb mode overnight so alerts don't interrupt sleep.
Reduce unnecessary notifications on apps so they don't interrupt sleep.
Try charging a device away from the bed instead of on the nightstand.
If a phone is needed as an alarm, consider placing it farther away so it's less tempting to check.
Try reducing highly stimulating or stressful content close to bedtime and notice whether it affects sleep.
Use night settings or reduced brightness on screens if they make viewing more comfortable, while understanding this doesn't address other factors like notifications or engaging content.

AAP Recommendation: The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends avoiding screen exposure for about an hour before bedtime and keeping devices out of bedrooms at night when practical. This reflects both the research on light and sleep and the broader goal of protecting sleep opportunity.

NOTICE WHAT'S BEING DISPLACED

A useful question is not simply "Was a screen used?" but rather whether digital use regularly interferes with things that matter:

Consider whether digital use is regularly interfering with:
• Sleep (duration or quality)
• School or work
• Physical activity or movement
• Relationships with friends or family
• Meals or mealtimes
• Responsibilities at home or school
• Hobbies or offline activities someone values

Context matters. Someone spending several hours creating digital art, completing schoolwork, talking with distant family, or using accessibility tools is experiencing something different from someone staying awake several hours longer than intended scrolling. The question is whether the time and activity are aligned with what the person wants or needs to do.

WHEN DIGITAL USE STARTS INTERFERING WITH DAILY LIFE

Sometimes digital use becomes a concern when it starts getting in the way of daily life or causing distress. Possible signs worth paying attention to include:

• Repeatedly staying online longer than intended, even when someone wants to stop
• Regularly losing sleep because of device use, and it happening often enough to be noticeable
• Difficulty stopping or reducing use even when someone wants to
• Missing important responsibilities, schoolwork, or commitments because of use
• Significant conflict or distress around use—either during use or when trying to stop
• Important relationships or activities being displaced or neglected
• Feeling that the use is no longer enjoyable but continuing anyway

One sign by itself does not necessarily mean digital use is a problem. Context matters. If digital use is causing persistent distress or significantly interfering with daily functioning, consider talking with a pediatrician, doctor, mental-health professional, or other appropriate healthcare professional. They can provide personalized guidance based on individual circumstances.

FOR PARENTS, CAREGIVERS & EDUCATORS
There is no universal hour limit that applies to every person or household.

Different families have different needs and circumstances. What works for one household may not work for another. Consider:

• Age and developmental stage
• Sleep needs and school schedule
• School, work, or learning responsibilities
• Social connection and hobbies
• Physical activity needs
• Accessibility needs (assistive technology, communication tools)
• What the digital use is for
• Whether use is causing problems

When developmentally appropriate, collaborative discussion can be more effective than one-sided rules. Possible conversation starters:

Questions to discuss together:
• What do you like doing online?
• Is anything online making it harder to sleep?
• Do notifications wake you up at night?
• Are there nights when you stay online longer than you mean to?
• What evening routine would feel realistic for you?
• Is there anything we could change as a household?

Adults can model the same habits they are discussing with young people. If device-free mealtimes or screen-free bedrooms are the goal, parents and other adults should participate too.

TRY IT FOR A WEEK

Experimentation can help identify what actually makes a difference. This approach reinforces learning and observation rather than punishment.

Choose ONE change to try:
Turn on Do Not Disturb overnight
Stop nonessential screen use about an hour before bed
Charge the phone farther from the bed
Turn off unnecessary nighttime notifications
Choose calmer content before bed
Other: _______________________________
Before the week: How is sleep going now?

After a week:
• Did falling asleep change?
• Did nighttime waking change?
• Did morning tiredness change?
• Did the change feel realistic?

This experiment reinforces observation and individual differences rather than assuming a change will work the same way for everyone.
