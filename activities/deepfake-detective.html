🔍 Deepfake Detective

The goal is not to become a human AI detector. The goal is to become better at verification.

Practice
Verification skills
Time
About 10–15 minutes
Works for
Yourself, families, groups, educators

What You'll Practice

You'll decide what to VERIFY when you encounter questionable online content. Rather than looking for visual proof, you'll practice checking sources, context, and independent reporting.

A realistic image is not proof that something happened. A strange-looking image is not proof that AI made it. The practical skill is knowing where to look for real evidence.

Verification Framework

When you encounter content you're unsure about, follow this order:

  1. Source — Where did this come from originally?
  2. Provenance — Can you trace its history?
  3. Corroboration — Do other reliable sources confirm it?
  4. Context — When and where was this supposed to happen?
  5. Content clues — What details can you check?
  6. Tools — Are there reverse-image searches or fact-checkers?

Try It

For each scenario, think about what you would actually check before believing or sharing it. Select your choice, then see the reasoning.

Scenario 1
A dramatic photo is circulating on social media. It supposedly shows something that happened in your city today. You don't recognize the source, but the image looks realistic and has already been shared hundreds of times.

Good reasons to verify:

Find the original source — Photos get reposted constantly. Finding who originally posted it tells you a lot. Did it come from a news outlet? A social media account? Someone claiming to be there?

Check for independent reporting — If something significant happened today, local news should be covering it. If no news outlet is reporting it, that's a strong clue.

Look at details — This is useful, but not as proof. Strange details might mean the image is AI-generated, but they might also mean poor lighting, a camera glitch, or something that just looks odd to you.

Sharing because it spread widely — This is a common trap. Things spread on social media regardless of whether they're true.

The key question: Can you find independent confirmation? If not, wait.

Scenario 2
A video clip appears to show a public figure saying something shocking. It's edited to be short, no context is given, and you can't find it on the figure's official accounts or channels.

Good reasons to verify:

Find the full original video — Short clips without context are especially risky. The context before or after the clip might completely change the meaning.

Check official accounts — If it's a real statement, the person or their official channels should have it. If they deny it or clarify it, that's important information.

Look for news coverage — Major statements by public figures get reported. If this is real and significant, news outlets should be covering it.

Sharing without verification — Even if it turns out to be real, sharing unverified clips spreads incomplete information and fuels confusion.

The key point: Context matters. A short clip without the full story is inherently unreliable.

Scenario 3
Someone points out distorted hands or strange text in an image and says: "See? This is definitely AI. AI always messes those things up." The image otherwise looks completely realistic.

Here's the nuance:

Visual flaws as proof — This is a common trap. Hands or text might be distorted because of compression, camera angles, poor lighting, or photo editing. Visual flaws are a clue worth investigating, but they're not proof.

Use visual flaws as one clue — This is better. Strange details can be worth noting, but they should be combined with source checks and corroboration.

Assuming it's real because it looks mostly normal — This is also incomplete. Realistic appearance doesn't prove anything either.

The key insight: AI detection from visual clues alone is unreliable. Check the story, not just the image.

Scenario 4
An image looks completely realistic with no obvious visual mistakes. Someone says: "It looks real, so it must be real."

Why appearance doesn't tell the whole story:

Trust it because it looks real — This misses the point. Both AI-generated and human-created images can look realistic. Appearance alone isn't evidence.

Check the source and context — This is the right approach. Regardless of what the image looks like, verify where it came from, who posted it, and what independent sources say about the claim.

AI detector tools — These exist, but they're not reliable. They give false positives and false negatives. They're one tool among many, not definitive proof.

The bigger point: AI-generated doesn't automatically mean false. Human-created doesn't automatically mean true. Check the actual claim, not just the medium.
