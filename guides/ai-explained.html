AI Explained: What It Is and What It Isn't

AI can do some pretty impressive things, but it isn't magic and it doesn't always get things right. Understanding a few basics can make it much easier to use.

Reviewed September 2026 · Sources

What AI is and types of AI

Artificial intelligence, or AI, is a broad term for computer systems designed to do things like recognize patterns, make predictions, generate content, recommend information or help make decisions.

You probably already use AI in ways you don't think much about. Spam filters, recommendations, navigation, photo organization and voice assistants can all involve AI.

Generative AI

Generative AI is a type of AI that creates new content, including text, images, audio, video and computer code.

Tools like ChatGPT and Gemini are examples of generative AI, but they aren't the only kind of AI.

Put simply: AI is the bigger category. Generative AI is one type of AI.

How generative AI works

AI models are trained using large amounts of data so they can identify patterns.

A large language model, or LLM, learns patterns in language. When you give it a prompt, it uses those patterns to generate a response.

People often say AI "learns," but that doesn't necessarily mean it learns the way a person does.

It can produce remarkably human-sounding language without that being proof that it thinks, understands or experiences the world the way you do.

Why people use AI

AI can be genuinely useful.

People use it to:

  • brainstorm ideas
  • explain complicated information
  • organize thoughts
  • translate or simplify language
  • practice skills
  • write and understand computer code
  • create images and other content
  • make information more accessible
  • summarize material
  • compare options
  • get past the dreaded blank page

It can save time and help people do things they may have struggled to do on their own.

What you should know about AI limitations

AI can sound confident and still be wrong

Generative AI creates responses based on patterns. It does not automatically verify every statement before presenting it.

Sometimes it produces information that is false, made up, inconsistent or unsupported. This is commonly called an AI hallucination or confabulation.

Key principle

Confidence is not evidence. A polished, detailed answer can still contain a mistake.

AI can also agree with you too much

AI assistants are often designed to be helpful and cooperative. Sometimes that can turn into excessive agreement, often called sycophancy.

An AI agreeing with you doesn't necessarily mean your idea is good or your conclusion is correct.

This matters especially when you're asking for advice or working through something emotional.

Try asking:

  • What might I be missing?
  • What assumptions am I making?
  • Give me the strongest argument against this.
  • What information could change this conclusion?

We can trust AI too much, too

There's another issue called automation bias: our tendency to give an automated answer more weight simply because a system produced it.

These are three different things worth remembering:

  • AI can be wrong: Hallucination or confabulation
  • AI can agree too much: Sycophancy
  • We can trust it too much: Automation bias
Making choices about AI use

You don't have to choose between trusting AI completely and never using it.

Think about what you're asking AI to do.

Brainstorming birthday-party themes has very different consequences from making a medical, legal or financial decision.

The more important the outcome, the more important it becomes to verify information and involve an appropriate human expert when needed.

The YOU → AI → YOU framework

Try thinking of AI as part of a process:

YOU → AI → YOU

You: Decide what you're trying to accomplish.

AI: Help explain, brainstorm, organize, generate or explore.

You: Review the result, question it, correct it and decide what to use.

AI can help you think. It doesn't have to do the thinking for you.

Put it in context

AI doesn't have to be something you fear or something you trust completely.

It can be incredibly useful without being infallible.

Understanding how it works makes it easier to know when to use it, when to question it and when the final decision should stay with you.
