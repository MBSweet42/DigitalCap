Voice Cloning & Impersonation Scams

A voice can sound exactly like someone you know and still be fake. Learn how voice cloning works, what scammers are trying to do, and how to verify before you act.

Last reviewed: September 2026

What is this?

Voice cloning uses AI to create a synthetic copy of someone's voice. In some cases, the technology can work from a relatively short audio sample.

Voice cloning itself has legitimate uses—accessibility, entertainment, creative projects—but it can also be used to impersonate someone.

When scammers use voice cloning, the goal is usually:

  • Pretending to be someone you know (family member, friend, colleague)
  • Pretending to be an authority figure (government official, employer, bank)
  • Creating fake calls that sound urgent or emotional
  • Requesting money, passwords, codes, or sensitive information

The scammer counts on:

  • The familiar voice triggering automatic trust
  • The emotional pressure of the situation (emergency, urgency)
  • Your instinct to help quickly without verifying

Why should I know about this?

A voice that sounds exactly like someone you know is no longer enough, by itself, to prove that person is calling.

This matters especially when the message involves:

  • An emergency (medical, legal, accident)
  • Money (bills, investments, payments)
  • Secrecy ("don't tell anyone")
  • Passwords or codes
  • Urgent instructions
  • Unusual requests

The FTC has documented scammers using voice cloning to impersonate family members in emergency situations, requesting urgent payment for medical bills, bail, or car accidents.

The FBI has documented malicious campaigns using AI voice to impersonate senior U.S. officials and to request authentication codes through vishing (voice phishing) tactics.

What should I know?

Listen for these pressure tactics

Scammers using voice cloning often rely on:

  • Urgency – "I need this money in the next hour"
  • Secrecy – "Don't tell anyone about this"
  • Emotional pressure – Fear, shame, or panic about the situation
  • Authority – Acting as someone with power or authority
  • Isolation – Asking you not to verify by calling back or asking someone else

These are familiar scam tactics, whether the voice is synthetic or real.

Workplace impersonation

Scammers may clone a boss's or executive's voice to request payment for unexpected expenses, wire transfers, or sensitive information. Same tactic: urgency, emotional pressure, request for secrecy.

Family code words don't guarantee security

Some families use code words as a safety practice ("I'll text you a code word to prove it's me"). This can help, but it's not foolproof—code words can be guessed, overheard, or socially engineered.

Independent verification is stronger.

A familiar voice is not verification.

What can I do?

Use this framework if someone calls with an unexpected request:

STOP → VERIFY ELSEWHERE → THEN ACT

STOP
Don't immediately comply, especially if the request involves money, passwords, or urgent action.

VERIFY ELSEWHERE
Contact the person or organization using a phone number, email, or website you know is theirs—not the contact information the caller gave you. Use a phone number from an official document, your own records, or a directory.

THEN ACT
After you've verified through an independent channel, then take action if the request is real.

For example, if someone claiming to be your bank calls about fraud:

  • Hang up
  • Call your bank using the number on your debit card or bank statement
  • Ask if there was fraud on your account
  • Then handle any necessary action

If someone claiming to be a family member calls with an emergency:

  • Don't send money immediately
  • Call that family member back on their phone number (from your phone, not a number the caller gave)
  • Ask directly: Is this really you? Is this emergency real?
  • Then help if the emergency is real

The voice can be fake. Verification should happen outside the conversation.

If you already sent money

If you realize you were scammed after sending money:

Act quickly

  • Contact your bank or payment service immediately. Ask if the transaction can be reversed or if the recipient's account can be frozen.
  • Report the fraud to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. This helps the FTC track scam trends.
  • Report internet-based fraud to the FBI at ic3.gov (Internet Crime Complaint Center).

Report the incident to local law enforcement if requested. Save all evidence: phone records, messages, call logs, screenshots.

Put it in context

Scammers have always used urgency, emotional pressure, and impersonation.

Voice cloning is a new tool that makes the impersonation more convincing, not a fundamentally new threat.

The response is the same:

When something feels urgent or unusual, verify it through an independent channel before complying.

You don't have to be an expert at spotting fake voices. You just need to follow one simple rule: if money or sensitive information is involved, verify outside the conversation before acting.
