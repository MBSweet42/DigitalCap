Build Your Family's Digital Agreement
A flexible worksheet for talking about devices, accounts, privacy, gaming, purchases, and what to do when something goes wrong.

There isn't one set of digital rules that works for every household. Families have different needs, values, and comfort levels. This worksheet provides areas to discuss and areas to write down what you agree on. The key is clarity and communication—and revisiting the agreement as circumstances and age change.

You don't need to fill this out all at once. Pick topics that matter most to your household, have the conversation, and write down what you decide. The agreement can change as your needs change.

WHEN AGREEMENTS ARE BROKEN
When an agreement isn't followed, there's usually a reason to address it. But the response depends on what happened and what makes sense for your family. Different families handle this differently, and that's okay.
If an agreement is broken, what response makes sense?
• A conversation about what happened?
• A temporary change in settings or access?
• Paying back a cost (for unauthorized purchases)?
• Repairing harm (apologizing, taking responsibility)?
• Revisiting whether the rule is realistic or whether supervision needs to change?
Our approach:
Principle:

DigitalCap doesn't recommend one consequence system for every family. What matters is being clear about what happens if an agreement is broken, and having that conversation before a problem occurs so expectations are understood.

Tips for Making This Work
  • Create expectations together when possible. People are more likely to follow agreements they helped create.
  • Start with what matters most. You don't need to fill out every section at once. Pick 2-3 areas and build from there.
  • Adults can model the habits you're discussing. If device-free family time is the goal, parents should participate too.
  • Revisit agreements regularly. Plans may need to change as kids get older, as new devices arrive, or as circumstances shift.
  • Clarity is often more useful than assumptions. A conversation about what you each expect is worth more than guessing.
  • Rules should help support safety and healthy use, not make someone afraid to ask for help. The point is connection and understanding, not control.
