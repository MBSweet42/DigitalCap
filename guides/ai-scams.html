AI Scams: What Changes and What Doesn't

AI can make scams look, sound and feel more convincing. But many of the tactics scammers use are still very familiar.

Reviewed September 2026 · Sources

The quick version

What you need to know:

AI makes scams more convincing but doesn't change the underlying manipulation

Fake voices, photos, videos, and profiles are more realistic. But scammers still use urgency, secrecy, emotional pressure, and requests for unusual payment.

Watch the behavior, not just the technology

Is someone pressuring you to act quickly? Asking for secrecy? Requesting unusual payment or credentials? Blocking verification? These warning signs work whether the scam is high-tech or low-tech.

Use STOP → VERIFY ELSEWHERE → THEN ACT

Don't immediately respond to urgent requests involving money or credentials. Verify through an independent channel first using contact information you already have.

If you're pressured or extorted, don't pay and report it

Paying extortionists doesn't stop them—victims report continued demands. Report to the FBI's IC3 at ic3.gov and to the platform.

How AI changes what scams look and sound like

AI can be used in scams to create convincing fake content: fake voices (voice cloning), fake photos/videos/deepfakes, fake messages/text, and convincing fake identities and profiles.

The technology itself isn't new—scams are as old as communication. What's new is how convincing the fake content can be.

Photos and video

AI can generate entirely synthetic photos or videos, or alter real ones. A deepfake video of someone saying something they never said can look remarkably real. A fake photo of a public figure, event, or product can spread quickly before fact-checking catches up.

Voice

Voice cloning can create a synthetic copy of someone's voice using just a short audio sample. A call from someone who sounds exactly like your parent, boss, or friend can feel very real.

Text and messages

AI can generate convincing text messages, emails, and social media posts that match someone's writing style or impersonate an authority figure.

Identities and profiles

AI can create fake dating profiles, social media accounts, and online identities with generated photos, convincing stories, and realistic conversation patterns.

The result: Scams that look, sound, and feel more convincing than before.

What pressure tactics still look familiar

Despite the new tools, scammers still use the same tactics to pressure people:

Urgency

Act now. This won't wait. You have only hours. This is time-limited.

Secrecy

Don't tell anyone. Keep this private. Don't ask anyone else about it.

Emotional pressure

Fear, shame, embarrassment, guilt, panic, love, excitement—whatever emotion motivates action without thinking.

Authority

Impersonating a boss, government official, bank, legal authority, or someone in power.

Isolation

Asking you not to verify the story by calling back, asking someone else, or checking independent sources.

Unusual payment requests

Asking for payment by wire transfer, gift cards, cryptocurrency, or other hard-to-reverse methods.

Requests for credentials or codes

Asking for passwords, authentication codes, or security information.

These warning signs work whether the threat comes through a realistic deepfake video or a simple text message.

Specific types of AI scams

Romance scams and AI

The FTC reports that romance scams have become more convincing with AI.

Scammers create fake profiles with AI-generated photos, write compelling messages, develop relationships over weeks or months, and eventually ask for money for emergencies, travel, investment opportunities, or to cover debts.

Warning signs still apply:

  • The person can never meet in person (always excuses)
  • Escalating declarations of love and trust-building
  • Eventually requests for money

AI makes the profile photos and messages more convincing. The manipulation tactics are the same.

Sextortion and manipulated images

Sextortion scams use fake or manipulated intimate images to threaten and extort money from victims.

This can involve:

  • AI-generated fake explicit images created from ordinary social media photos
  • Actual intimate images obtained through social engineering or hacking
  • Deepfake or edited versions of real images

The extortionist threatens to send the fake images to friends, family, or employer unless the victim pays.

The fact that an image is fake does not make the threat or harm fake.

Real extortion is happening, even if the images are synthetic. The FBI reports that victims who pay are often targeted again by the same extortionist or by copycat criminals.

Emergency scams

Scammers impersonate family members, employers, or officials claiming an emergency (accident, medical crisis, legal problem, arrest) that requires immediate payment.

AI voice cloning makes these scams more convincing. But the manipulation is still:

  • Urgency and panic
  • Emotional pressure
  • Requests for secrecy ("don't tell anyone")
  • Isolation from verification
What to do if you're pressured or extorted

When something urgent or suspicious comes your way

Use this framework:

STOP → VERIFY ELSEWHERE → THEN ACT

STOP
Don't immediately respond or comply, especially if the request involves money, passwords, credentials, or sensitive information.

VERIFY ELSEWHERE
Contact the person or organization independently. Use a phone number from a document you trust, a website you know is official, or contact information from your own records—not the contact information the message provided.

THEN ACT
After you've verified through an independent channel, then decide how to respond.

You do not have to prove someone is using AI before refusing to comply or share information.

I'm going to verify this first.

That's a complete sentence. You don't need to explain why, or prove anything, or commit to acting on the request. Verify somewhere else before money, credentials or sensitive information move anywhere.

If you're being sextorted or image-based threatened

If someone is threatening to share intimate images (real or fake) unless you pay:

  • Do not pay. Paying does not stop the extortion—victims report continued demands.
  • Do not engage with the extortionist. Block them and do not respond.
  • Report the threat to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov.
  • Report to the platform where the threat originated.

If the image was taken when you were under 18, NCMEC's Take It Down service may be able to help. If the person shown is over 18 in the image, StopNCII may be another option. These services also accept qualifying manipulated or deepfake intimate images. Both have eligibility requirements and work with participating platforms.

Other scams and fraud

  • Hang up or stop communication immediately.
  • Report the scam to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
  • Report to the FBI at ic3.gov.
  • If money was involved, contact your bank or payment service immediately to see if the transaction can be reversed.
Put it in context

AI is a tool. It can make scams more convincing. But the fundamental threat isn't new.

Scammers have always:

  • Used urgency to bypass thinking
  • Created fake identities and relationships
  • Impersonated authority or trusted people
  • Asked for money or sensitive information
  • Isolated victims from verification

AI makes the fake more convincing. It doesn't change how to respond.

Strong emotional pressure is a reason to slow down, not speed up.

When something triggers fear, panic, shame, guilt, or excitement, that's when you most need to verify independently before acting.

That's not paranoia. That's how you stay safe.
